**Kimi K3.1 Pro** is the first model in Moonshot AI's new K3.1 series. Moonshot calls it the most capable 3T-class open-weight model to date, and it has a **1-million-token context window**.

It brings substantial improvements in coding, 3D creation, financial research and other agentic capabilities.

Moonshot is also releasing **Kimi K3.1 Pro Highspeed**, which runs 2–3× faster than K3.1 Pro for more responsive interactions.

Kimi K3.1 Pro and Kimi K3.1 Pro Highspeed are available today on Kimi.ai, Kimi Work, Kimi Code and the Kimi API. The full model weights will be released by **November 4**.

## Kimi K3.1 Pro GPU Memory: How Many H100s Does It Take to Load 3T Parameters?

<div class="mem-box" markdown="1">

### Back-of-the-envelope: memory to load Kimi K3.1 Pro weights

**Weight memory = parameters × bytes per parameter**

With 3 trillion parameters (1 GB = 10⁹ bytes), every byte per parameter costs **3,000 GB**. One NVIDIA H100 has **80 GB**, so:

**H100s needed = weight memory ÷ 80 GB**, rounded up.

| Precision | Bytes / param | Weight memory | H100s (80 GB) | 8× H100 nodes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| FP32 | 4 | 3T × 4 B = **12,000 GB** (12 TB) | 12,000 ÷ 80 = **150** | 19 nodes (152 GPUs) |
| FP16 | 2 | 3T × 2 B = **6,000 GB** | 6,000 ÷ 80 = **75** | 10 nodes (80 GPUs) |
| BF16 | 2 | 3T × 2 B = **6,000 GB** | 6,000 ÷ 80 = **75** | 10 nodes (80 GPUs) |
| FP8 | 1 | 3T × 1 B = **3,000 GB** | 3,000 ÷ 80 = 37.5 → **38** | 5 nodes (40 GPUs) |
| INT8 | 1 | 3T × 1 B = **3,000 GB** | 3,000 ÷ 80 = 37.5 → **38** | 5 nodes (40 GPUs) |
| FP4 | 0.5 | 3T × 0.5 B = **1,500 GB** | 1,500 ÷ 80 = 18.75 → **19** | 3 nodes (24 GPUs) |
| INT4 | 0.5 | 3T × 0.5 B = **1,500 GB** | 1,500 ÷ 80 = 18.75 → **19** | 3 nodes (24 GPUs) |

**Reading the numbers**

- **FP32 is 4 bytes, not 8.** 8 bytes per parameter is FP64: 24,000 GB, or 300 H100s. Nobody serves an LLM in FP64.
- **FP16 and BF16 cost the same memory.** Both are 16 bits; BF16 just spends more bits on range and fewer on precision. The same holds for FP8 vs INT8 and FP4 vs INT4.
- **Each halving of precision halves the hardware bill.** Going from BF16 to INT4 drops Kimi K3.1 Pro from 10 nodes to 3.
- **These are minimums for the weights alone.** Real serving also needs room for the KV cache (large at a 1M-token context), activations and the CUDA runtime, and an H100 has a little less than 80 GB usable.
- **4-bit formats carry scale overhead.** Block-scaled FP4 (e.g. NVFP4, one FP8 scale per 16 values) is about 4.5 bits per weight: ~1,690 GB, or 22 H100s, which still fits in 3 nodes.

### Long-Horizon Coding: Teaching a Simulated Human to Walk

K3.1 Pro's long-horizon coding also improves through large-scale reinforcement learning.

In one internal test, it built a **human walking dynamics simulator** with integrated training and evaluation pipelines. It was built on top of **human-atlas**, an open-source anatomical model of the human body with 296 skeletal components (including cartilage) and 402 muscle components.

Across **167 missions and 420 independent reviews**, it analyzed experimental results, diagnosed problems and iteratively refined the walking controller. The simulated human went from collapsing within seconds to **walking continuously with alternating strides for 11.2 seconds**.

The most interesting part is what happened when reward shaping failed to improve arm motion. K3.1 Pro didn't just keep adjusting the reward. It:

1. ran **open-loop probes** of the simulator's dynamics,
2. found and corrected **36 errors in the shoulder-muscle moment-arm calculations**, and
3. retrained the controller.

## Kimi K3.1 Pro for Robotics: Humanoid Simulation Training vs NVIDIA

That walking experiment is a robotics result disguised as a coding demo. It is the daily loop of a humanoid-locomotion engineer: build the sim, train a policy, watch it fall over, work out why, fix it, retrain.

### What NVIDIA Is Doing for Humanoid Robots

NVIDIA's approach to humanoid robots is to own the infrastructure around the policy:

- **Isaac Sim**: the Omniverse/OpenUSD-based robot simulator, with sensors, rendering and PhysX physics.
- **Isaac Lab**: the RL and imitation-learning framework on top of Isaac Sim. It runs thousands of environments in parallel on one GPU, with ready-made humanoid locomotion tasks.
- **Newton**: an open-source, GPU-accelerated physics engine built on NVIDIA Warp, co-developed with Google DeepMind and Disney Research.
- **GR00T**: open humanoid foundation models, plus pipelines for generating synthetic training data.
- **Cosmos**: world foundation models used to generate and augment training data.

NVIDIA has also used LLMs inside this loop. **Eureka** (2023) had an LLM write reward functions, train policies in Isaac Gym, read the results and rewrite the reward. That loop taught a simulated robot hand to spin a pen.

### Where Kimi K3.1 Pro Goes Further Than Reward Search

Eureka-style systems treat the simulator as ground truth and search over reward code. The K3.1 Pro run shows the step after that: when reward changes stopped working, the model **suspected the simulator itself**, tested it with open-loop probes and fixed the physics model.

That matters because a wrong moment arm can't be fixed with reward tuning. The policy will happily learn to exploit a broken body model, and the result won't transfer to the real world.

| | NVIDIA stack | Kimi K3.1 Pro |
|---|---|---|
| Role | Simulation, physics, data and foundation policies | Agentic engineer that writes and debugs the pipeline |
| Scale lever | Thousands of parallel GPU environments | Hundreds of missions and reviews over a long horizon |
| Typical LLM use | Reward design (Eureka) | Reward design **plus** simulator debugging |
| Body model | Rigid articulated robots (URDF/MJCF → USD) | Musculoskeletal human (296 bones, 402 muscles) |
| Output | Trained policies, synthetic data | Working code, diagnoses, fixed models, retrained controllers |

They aren't competitors. NVIDIA supplies the gym; K3.1 Pro is a candidate for the engineer working in it.

### How Kimi K3.1 Pro Could Plug Into Isaac Sim and Isaac Lab

Here is how the walking experiment would map onto NVIDIA's stack, with K3.1 Pro driving through the Kimi API or Kimi Code:

1. **Import the body.** Convert the robot or body model into USD with Isaac Sim's URDF/MJCF importers. K3.1 Pro checks joint limits, masses and inertia after import, which is where conversions silently go wrong.
2. **Write the environment.** Generate an Isaac Lab environment config (observations, actions, reward terms, terminations, domain randomization), starting from an existing humanoid locomotion task.
3. **Train headless.** Launch Isaac Lab's RL training scripts (e.g. RSL-RL PPO) in headless mode across thousands of parallel environments.
4. **Read the results.** Parse the training logs and evaluation rollouts: episode length, fall rate, gait symmetry, torque usage.
5. **Probe the physics.** When reward changes plateau, run open-loop tests: apply known torques or activations, log the resulting motion and compare it to the expected values. This is the step that caught the 36 moment-arm errors.
6. **Fix, retrain, review.** Patch the model or environment, retrain, and keep a log of every change, like the 420 independent reviews in Moonshot's test.

One caveat: Isaac Sim's PhysX articulations are driven by joint torques, not muscles. A 402-muscle model like human-atlas would need a custom muscle-to-torque actuator layer, or a MuJoCo-style backend (MuJoCo is where musculoskeletal work such as MyoSuite usually runs).

The open K3.1 Pro weights (due by November 4) make on-prem use realistic: a robotics team could run the agent next to its Isaac Sim cluster without sending proprietary robot models to a third-party API.

## Kimi K3.1 Pro for 3D Modeling and CAD Design

K3.1 Pro is strong at 3D creation and spatial reasoning.

On **BenchCAD**, which tests 3D reconstruction from multi-view renderings through CAD code generation, K3.1 Pro scores **92.7% with tools**. That approaches Claude Opus 5.5 (96.2%) and GPT-6 Astra (95.9%).

From a text brief, sketch or reference image, it can infer shape, proportions and spatial relationships to create usable 3D models. It also has a stronger grasp of existing asset libraries and model collections.

### Why CAD Code Generation Matters for Mechanical Engineering

"CAD code generation" means the model doesn't output a mesh. It writes a **parametric program** that builds the part, in a code-CAD library such as CadQuery or build123d.

```python
import cadquery as cq

# 80 x 40 x 10 mm mounting plate with two 8 mm bolt holes
plate = (
    cq.Workplane("XY")
    .box(80, 40, 10)
    .faces(">Z").workplane()
    .pushPoints([(-25, 0), (25, 0)])
    .hole(8)
)
cq.exporters.export(plate, "plate.step")
```

For an engineer, this beats a pretty mesh:

- **Dimensions are explicit.** Every length is a number you can read, check and change.
- **Design intent survives.** Change the plate width and the holes stay where they should be.
- **The output is a real solid.** A STEP file opens in SolidWorks, AutoCAD, Fusion or ANSYS, not just a game engine.

### Kimi K3.1 Pro With SolidWorks, AutoCAD and ANSYS

Each of these tools already has a scripting interface, which is what an agentic model needs:

| Tool | Scripting interface | What Kimi K3.1 Pro could automate |
|---|---|---|
| **SolidWorks** | SolidWorks API (VBA macros, C#/VB.NET) | Building parts and assemblies from a brief, configurations, design tables, batch drawing export |
| **AutoCAD** | AutoLISP, .NET API, script (.scr) files | 2D drawings and layouts, block libraries, repetitive drafting and cleanup |
| **ANSYS** | PyAnsys (PyMAPDL, PyMechanical), APDL, Workbench journaling | Meshing, loads and boundary conditions, running FEA/CFD, pulling stress and deflection results |

### A Design–Simulate–Refine Loop for Engineers

Put together, this gives mechanical engineering the same loop K3.1 Pro ran on the walking simulator:

1. **Brief → model.** Turn a text brief, hand sketch or reference photo into a parametric CadQuery or SolidWorks model.
2. **Model → STEP.** Export a solid that drops into the existing CAD and PDM workflow.
3. **STEP → simulation.** Mesh and solve in ANSYS through PyMAPDL: static stress, modal or thermal.
4. **Results → diagnosis.** Read peak von Mises stress, safety factor and deflection, and find where the part fails.
5. **Refine → re-run.** Change the parameters (wall thickness, fillet radius, rib count) and repeat until the targets are met.

The walking experiment suggests the most valuable step: when a simulation result looks wrong, check the setup (units, contacts, constraints, mesh quality) before redesigning the part.

### Limitations for Production Engineering

- **BenchCAD measures shape, not engineering.** Rebuilding geometry from renderings says nothing about tolerances, GD&T, material choice or whether the part can be manufactured.
- **92.7% isn't 100%.** A missed chamfer or a wrong hole position is a scrapped part. Every generated model still needs a drawing review.
- **Simulation is only as good as its setup.** An agent can run ANSYS, but loads, boundary conditions and failure criteria still need an engineer's sign-off.
- **Licenses and data.** ANSYS and SolidWorks runs need licensed seats, and proprietary designs may not be allowed to leave the network. Open weights help with the second point.
