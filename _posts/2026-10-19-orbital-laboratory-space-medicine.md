*Space as a living laboratory for discovering new treatments, understanding human biology, and solving medical challenges on Earth.*

![View from inside the International Space Station Cupola: seven windows framing Earth's cloud-covered surface and a sunrise on the horizon, the orbital laboratory for space medicine research](/assets/blogs/orbital-laboratory-space-medicine/hero.jpeg)

Some of the most important space medicine research isn't about getting astronauts to Mars. It's about the patients waiting here on Earth.

About 400 km overhead, the International Space Station works as an orbital laboratory where gravity, the one constant in every lab on Earth, is effectively switched off. Without it, fluids, proteins, and cells behave differently, and sometimes that makes the work easier.

This post starts with one experiment, LambdaVision-1, which builds artificial retinas in orbit. Then it widens out to mini hearts and brain organoids grown on the ISS, and ends with the harder question: how do you keep a lab like this supplied, and get its products back home?

## The Problem Space Medicine Research Is Trying to Solve: Blindness With No Cure

Millions of people worldwide live with retinal degenerative diseases such as **retinitis pigmentosa (RP)** and **age-related macular degeneration (AMD)**.

In both, the retina's photoreceptor cells, the ones that turn light into nerve signals, stop working. There is no cure. Today's treatments can only slow the decline. They can't bring vision back.

That leaves a clear unmet need: a retinal prosthesis that can restore *meaningful* vision.

### A Retina Made of Protein

LambdaVision, Inc. is building one. Its implant uses a light-activated protein called **bacteriorhodopsin** to do the job of the damaged photoreceptors.

![LambdaVision-1 protein-based artificial retina implant: a cross-section of the human eye showing where the implant sits at the back of the retina, with an inset of the small purple bacteriorhodopsin film resting on a gloved fingertip](/assets/blogs/orbital-laboratory-space-medicine/lambdavision-artificial-retina-implant.jpg)

*The LambdaVision artificial retina and where it would be placed in the eye. The whole implant fits on a fingertip. Image: NASA JSC2026E416052, credit LambdaVision.*

The design has some real advantages:

- **No external power.** The implant runs on the light that enters the eye.
- **No bulky hardware.** Nothing is worn on or outside the eye.
- **Higher potential resolution** than competing electrode-based implants.
- **Flexible and subretinal.** It sits beneath the retina, where the lost cells used to be.

### Why Gravity Gets in the Way

The implant is a thin film built **one layer at a time** using a method called *layer-by-layer electrostatic adsorption*. Each layer sticks to the one below it through electrical attraction between molecules.

For this to work, every layer has to be even. On Earth, gravity keeps fighting that in two ways:

- **Sedimentation:** heavier material in the solution sinks instead of staying evenly spread.
- **Aggregation:** proteins clump together in the solution before they reach the film.

One uneven layer is a small flaw. Repeat it across the whole stack and the film ends up less uniform and less stable, and in the end it performs worse as an implant.

## How LambdaVision-1 Uses Microgravity Research to Build Better Retinas

**Layer-by-Layer Assembly of Protein-Based Artificial Retinas in Microgravity (LambdaVision-1)** moves this manufacturing process onto the International Space Station.

The hypothesis is simple. If gravity causes the sedimentation and aggregation, then taking gravity away should reduce them and produce more uniform films.

### What the Experiment Measures

LambdaVision-1 studies how gravity affects four properties of the thin films:

1. **Protein distribution:** where the protein ends up across the film
2. **Uniformity:** how consistent each layer is
3. **Stability:** how well the film holds together over time
4. **Performance:** how well it works as an artificial retina

Films built on Earth serve as the control group. Comparing them with the films built in orbit isolates what gravity changes.

![Earth-produced LambdaVision-1 artificial retina control sample: a small purple protein thin film on a clear, taped substrate held between two fingers, built on the ground for comparison with retinas manufactured in microgravity](/assets/blogs/orbital-laboratory-space-medicine/lambdavision-artificial-retina-earth-control.jpg)

*An artificial retina made on Earth, kept as the ground control for the films built in orbit. Image: NASA JSC2026E416054, credit LambdaVision.*

### Inside the Space Tango Mambo Tray

The retinas are made aboard the ISS in a **Space Tango Mambo Tray**, a payload that automates the layer-by-layer delivery of the experiment's fluids.

The operations are almost hands-off:

1. The tray launches in soft stow, with no orientation constraint.
2. The crew installs it in Space Tango's Mambo facility.
3. Manufacturing runs **autonomously**, and cameras capture images throughout.
4. The crew removes the tray and stows it for **return to Earth**.

That last step is easy to skip past, but it's the one that matters most. The retinas only help anyone once they're back on the ground.

### Why This Matters on Earth

If microgravity really does produce more uniform and stable retinal implants, it could improve how they are developed and produced for patients on Earth.

It also points at something bigger than one product. The ISS isn't only a place to observe biology. It can be a **factory** for things that are hard to make under gravity.

## Why Just Retinas? What Else Microgravity Research Can Do

If gravity can spoil a thin protein film, it can also distort something far more complicated: how **living cells arrange themselves into 3D tissue**.

That's where space medicine research gets really interesting. Two more ISS investigations take the same idea, removing gravity to remove a distortion, and apply it to the heart and the brain.

### Stellar Spheroids: Growing Mini Hearts With Blood Vessels in Orbit

**The problem:** heart drugs need human heart tissue to be tested on. Lab-grown *cardiac spheroids*, tiny 3D balls of heart cells, are one of the best options. But spheroids made on Earth are hard to keep alive, and they may never develop the organization or maturity needed for reliable disease modeling and drug testing.

**Stellar Spheroids** (*To Study the Growth and Functionality of Vascular Cardiac Spheroids in Low Earth Orbit*) tests whether microgravity can help.

#### What's Inside a Cardiac Spheroid

Every cell starts as a **human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)**: an adult cell reprogrammed so it can become almost any cell type. Two types are made from them:

- **Cardiomyocytes:** the heart muscle cells that contract
- **Endothelial cells:** the cells that line blood vessels

Each spheroid combines **100,000 cardiomyocytes** (cell line 02i) with **8,000 endothelial cells**. The endothelial cells carry a red fluorescent marker (Tubulin-RFP), so researchers can watch them spread out and form vessel-like networks inside the tissue.

That vessel network is the real goal. Real heart tissue is threaded with blood vessels, and a model without them is missing something important.

![Stellar Spheroids cardiac spheroid under fluorescence microscopy: a glowing red sphere of hiPSC-derived heart cells with tube-like structures formed by red-labeled endothelial cells, 100 micrometer scale bar](/assets/blogs/orbital-laboratory-space-medicine/stellar-spheroids-cardiac-spheroid-vascular-tubes.jpg)

*An Earth-produced cardiac spheroid before flight. The red tube-like structures are formed by endothelial cells. Image: NASA JSC2026E416055, credit Arun Sharma laboratory, Cedars-Sinai Medical Center.*

#### How the Experiment Runs on the ISS

The setup is built for **throughput**, not just one-off results:

- **4** low-attachment 96-well plates, with **12** experimental wells each, giving **48** vascularized spheroids in total
- **Low-attachment** plastic stops cells sticking to the plate, so they clump together into 3D spheroids on their own
- **Matched Earth controls** run on the same timing and conditions
- An **~26-day culture**, monitored with brightfield imaging, fluorescence microscopy, and video to track how the spheroids contract

Why might microgravity help? Without gravity, cells float in suspension instead of settling. That could change how they spread out, how nutrients reach them, and how tightly the tissue packs together.

The research team is careful here: these effects are **to be tested, not assumed**. The experiment also builds on the 2023 **Stellar Stem Cells** mission on Axiom Mission 2, where hiPSCs quickly formed 3D aggregates in microgravity.

![Stellar Spheroids hiPSC-derived cardiomyocyte spheroid in a brightfield and fluorescence overlay: a red-labeled spheroid of heart muscle and endothelial cells against a grey culture background, 100 micrometer scale bar](/assets/blogs/orbital-laboratory-space-medicine/stellar-spheroids-cardiac-spheroid-endothelial-network.jpg)

*The same kind of spheroid in a brightfield and fluorescence overlay, showing the red endothelial cells inside the heart tissue. Image: NASA JSC2026E416056, credit Arun Sharma laboratory, Cedars-Sinai Medical Center.*

#### Four Plates, Four Ways to Read the Results

At the end of the mission, each plate is preserved differently so that one flight answers several questions:

| Plate | Preservation | What it reveals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Fixed in paraformaldehyde | Structure, shape, and vessel networks |
| 2 | Lysed in RLT buffer | Bulk RNA sequencing (gene activity) |
| 3 | Cryopreserved | Post-flight recovery, viability, and function |
| 4 | Snap-frozen | Single-nucleus RNA sequencing (cell by cell) |

The spent culture medium comes back too, for **proteomic and metabolomic** analysis of what the cells secreted.

#### Why It Matters

Space speeds up certain cellular stress and aging-like changes, and it exposes biological pathways that are hard to detect on Earth.

This first mission is meant to show that vascularized cardiac spheroids can be **maintained reliably in 96-well plates in space**. If that works, the next mission can use the same model for high-throughput testing of:

- **Cardioprotective** drugs, which shield the heart
- **Cardiotoxic** drugs, which can damage it
- **Chemotherapy** drugs, whose heart side effects matter for cancer patients

Further out, the same approach could produce **astronaut-specific heart models** for personalized risk assessment on long missions.

### MINDS: Running "Clinical Trials in a Dish" for Brain Disease

**The problem:** Parkinson's disease, primary progressive multiple sclerosis, and Alzheimer's disease are *not* developmental disorders. They unfold slowly over decades, which makes them very hard to recreate in a dish, even with modern cell reprogramming.

What they share is **neuroinflammation**: inflammation inside the brain. Studying it means studying how neurons interact with **microglia**, the brain's resident immune cells. Experiments on that interaction in microgravity have been limited.

**MINDS** (*Microgravity Integrated Neuro Disease Screening*) is designed to fill that gap.

#### Brain Organoids Built From Patients

MINDS grows **brain organoids**, small 3D models of brain tissue, from patient-derived iPSCs. Each organoid models the brain region a specific disease attacks:

| Disease | Organoid type |
| --- | --- |
| Parkinson's disease | Dopaminergic organoids |
| Primary progressive MS | Cortical organoids |
| Alzheimer's disease | Cholinergic organoids (new in MINDS) |

Each organoid combines neurons with microglia, so the inflammatory cross-talk between them actually takes place.

![MINDS brain organoid after 30 days on the International Space Station: phase-contrast microscope image of dense neurite outgrowth spreading from a cultured neural organoid on the right into a network of branching neurons on the left](/assets/blogs/orbital-laboratory-space-medicine/minds-brain-organoid-neurite-outgrowth.jpg)

*Neurites growing out of a brain organoid after 30 days on the ISS, showing how the cells interact. Image: NASA JSC2026E416047, credit Aspen Neuroscience.*

#### The Results That Came Before

This team ran the **first long-term cultures of patient-specific neural cells in low Earth orbit**. The first proof-of-principle study used four iPSC lines:

- One from a person with Parkinson's disease
- One from a person with primary progressive MS
- Two age- and sex-matched healthy controls

In orbit, both dopaminergic and cortical organoids showed **significant differences in gene expression and protein secretion** linked to maturation and neurodegenerative disease pathways.

Those results have been repeated, and a prior investigation, **HBOND** (*Human Brain Organoid Models for Neurodegenerative Disease & Drug Discovery*), validated drug testing in the model.

#### Cosmic Organoid Villages

This is the cleverest part of MINDS.

Instead of one organoid per patient, MINDS combines cells from **several disease-specific patient lines into a single organoid**, which it calls a *Cosmic Organoid Village*™.

One patient's cells tell you about one patient. A village represents the **diversity of a wider patient population**. Testing drugs on villages starts to look like a **"clinical trial in a dish"**.

![MINDS investigation dopaminergic neurons imaged with the KERMIT microscope aboard the International Space Station: green fluorescent neurons with a dashed box marking a region of interest, 200 micrometer scale bar](/assets/blogs/orbital-laboratory-space-medicine/minds-dopaminergic-neurons-kermit-microscope.jpg)

*Dopaminergic neurons, the cells lost in Parkinson's disease, imaged in microgravity with the KERMIT microscope aboard the ISS. Image: NASA JSC2026E416048, credit National Stem Cell Foundation Team.*

#### How MINDS Runs in Orbit

- **4 Plate Habitats (PHABs)**, each holding **80 cryovials** with one organoid per vial, for 320 organoids in total
- Kept at **ambient temperature** on the way up
- Held at **37 °C** in orbit in BioServe Space Technologies' **Space Automated Bioproduct Lab (SABL)**
- Exposed to microgravity for about **30 to 45 days**
- Returned to Earth **alive**, at ambient temperature, for post-flight analysis

The payoff runs both ways. On Earth, it could lead to better treatments and prevention for neurodegenerative disease. In space, it could support countermeasures that protect astronauts' brains on long missions.

### Three Experiments, One Pattern

| Investigation | What gravity distorts | What gets built in orbit | What must come home |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| LambdaVision-1 | Protein settling and clumping | Artificial retina films | The finished films |
| Stellar Spheroids | Cell settling in 3D culture | Vascularized heart spheroids | Fixed, frozen, and live plates plus culture medium |
| MINDS | Neural tissue modeling | Patient-derived brain organoids | Live organoids |

The pattern is clear: in each case, the science only counts once the samples are **back on Earth**.

## The Real Question: How Do We Sustain an Orbital Laboratory?

Three experiments make a strong case. But a pipeline of treatments that reaches patients needs much more:

- **Regular flights up**, carrying fresh materials, fluids, and hardware
- **Regular flights down**, bringing finished products back for testing and use
- **Gentle, temperature-controlled handling**, because protein films, frozen cell plates, and live organoids are all environmentally sensitive cargo
- **A cost that drops over time**, so in-space biomanufacturing makes economic sense

The second point is the hard part. Getting things *up* to orbit is a solved problem with many options. Getting delicate cargo *back down*, intact and in useful amounts, is much rarer.

An orbital laboratory is only as useful as its supply line. So what keeps that line running?

## SpaceX Dragon: The Supply Line for In-Space Biomanufacturing

A large part of the answer today is **SpaceX's Dragon** spacecraft.

![SpaceX Dragon spacecraft against a dark background: the white pressurized capsule with Draco thruster ports and the SpaceX logo, mounted on the dark blue trunk that carries cargo to the International Space Station](/assets/blogs/orbital-laboratory-space-medicine/spacex-dragon.png)

*SpaceX Dragon: the white pressurized capsule on top carries crew and sensitive cargo; the trunk below carries unpressurized cargo and solar panels.*

SpaceX describes Dragon as the only spacecraft currently flying that can **return significant amounts of cargo to Earth**. It was also the first private spacecraft to take humans to the space station.

That return capability is exactly the bottleneck an orbital lab runs into.

### Dragon by the Numbers

| Specification | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Height | 8.1 m (26.7 ft) |
| Diameter | 4 m (13 ft) |
| Pressurized spacecraft volume | 9.3 m³ (328 ft³) |
| Trunk volume | 37 m³ (1,300 ft³) |
| Launch payload mass | 6,000 kg (13,228 lbs) |
| Return payload mass | 3,000 kg (6,614 lbs) |
| Crew capacity | Up to 7 passengers |

The two cargo areas map neatly onto what space medicine research needs. The **pressurized capsule** carries people and environmentally sensitive cargo, such as retina films, preserved heart spheroids, and live brain organoids. The **trunk** carries unpressurized cargo on the way up.

### How Dragon Sustains Repeated Flights

A supply line has to be *repeatable*, and Dragon is built to fly again and again. According to SpaceX's live mission counter (as of October 2026):

- **57** completed missions
- **52** visits to the ISS
- **37** total reflights

Reflights are the key number. Historically, most of the cost of a launch came from building hardware that flew once. Flying the same capsule again spreads that cost across many missions, and that's what turns a one-off experiment into a schedule you can plan around.

### The Hardware That Makes It Work

Several systems work together to get Dragon up, keep it working in orbit, and bring it home:

- **Solar-powered trunk.** One half of the trunk is covered in solar panels that power Dragon in flight and while it's docked to the station. The trunk stays attached until shortly before reentry.
- **16 Draco thrusters.** These orient the spacecraft and handle orbit adjustments, apogee/perigee maneuvers, and attitude control. Each one produces 90 pounds of force in vacuum.
- **8 SuperDraco engines.** They power the launch escape system and can push Dragon half a mile away from the rocket in under eight seconds if something goes wrong.
- **Parachutes for a controlled return.** Two drogue parachutes stabilize the capsule after reentry, and four main parachutes slow it further before landing.

That controlled return is what lets fragile, experiment-grade cargo, like a stack of protein films or a habitat of living organoids, come back to Earth in usable condition.

## This Is Already Happening: SpaceX CRS-35 and the Orbital Laboratory

None of this is hypothetical. The three experiments in this post are **in orbit right now**.

On **October 15, 2026**, a Dragon spacecraft docked with the International Space Station on **SpaceX's 35th commercial resupply mission (CRS-35)** for NASA. It carried about **6,300 pounds** of cargo.

Along with food and supplies for the crew, it carried the materials and equipment for:

- **LambdaVision-1**, artificial retinas built layer by layer in microgravity
- **Stellar Spheroids**, 3D human heart tissue with vessel-like networks
- **MINDS**, patient-derived brain organoid "villages" for neurodegenerative disease research

### More Than Medicine: Powering the Lab Itself

The same flight also delivered **ISS Roll-Out Solar Arrays (IROSAs)**. According to NASA, these arrays complete a multi-year upgrade of the station's power system and increase how much power it can generate.

It's a small detail that says a lot. An orbital laboratory doesn't just need experiments flown up. It needs its **own infrastructure** kept up to date, and the same supply line handles both.

### The Round Trip Closes the Loop

Dragon is expected to stay docked for **about a month**. In **mid-November**, it's scheduled to undock and splash down in the **Pacific Ocean off the California coast**, bringing research samples and hardware back to Earth.

That's the part this whole post has been building toward. The retina films, the preserved heart spheroids, and the live brain organoids are only useful once they're home. CRS-35 is the round trip that makes these experiments count.

### A Lab With a Track Record

The ISS has hosted research in microgravity for **more than 25 years**. Scientists from **more than 110 countries** have run **more than 4,000 experiments** on board.

What's changing now is the *kind* of work flying up. It's moving from observing how biology behaves in space to **building medical products and disease models** that could only be made there, and NASA and SpaceX are already flying it on a regular schedule.

## Key Takeaways

- **Space medicine research can solve problems on Earth.** LambdaVision-1 targets blindness from RP and AMD, which have no cure.
- **Gravity can be a manufacturing defect.** Sedimentation and aggregation spoil layer-by-layer protein films. Microgravity may remove both.
- **The ISS can work as a factory, not just a lab.** Automated payloads like the Mambo Tray build products in orbit with very little crew time.
- **The idea reaches well beyond retinas.** Stellar Spheroids grows vascularized mini hearts for drug testing, and MINDS runs "clinical trials in a dish" on patient-derived brain organoids.
- **Bringing cargo back is the bottleneck.** In-space products only matter if they come home intact.
- **Reusability keeps the supply line running.** With 37 reflights across 57 missions, Dragon makes regular trips to orbit and back routine.
- **It's already happening.** SpaceX CRS-35 delivered all three experiments to the ISS in October 2026 and is due to bring the samples home in mid-November.

## Conclusion

LambdaVision-1, Stellar Spheroids, and MINDS share one big idea: some medical products, and some models of human disease, might be better made in orbit than on the ground.

But the science is only half the system. An orbital laboratory depends on its supply line, with materials going up, products coming down, and costs falling with every reflight. With CRS-35, NASA and SpaceX are already running that loop.

The open question isn't whether microgravity can make better medicine. It's whether we can fly there often enough, and cheaply enough, for patients to feel the difference.
